The idea in one minute

Science tells you what things are. It does not tell you what to do. Peterson argues that myths and religious stories are humanity's oldest answer to the second question. They are not failed science. They are maps of how to act, built up over thousands of years of watching what works.

Three claims carry the whole book.

1. Everyone lives inside a story: where I am, where I should be, how to get there. That story decides what you notice and what you feel.

2. Every story meets things it did not predict. Your nervous system treats the unexpected as threat and promise at once. Myths picture this as chaos, a dragon guarding treasure.

3. The right response is to face the unexpected voluntarily and update. Myths call the one who does this the hero. Refusing to look is the root of personal misery and, at scale, of political atrocity.

Peterson wrote it over about thirteen years while haunted by the Cold War: how could people be so sure of their beliefs that they would risk destroying the world for them? The book is his attempt at an answer.

The engine of the book

What happens when your map breaks

Peterson says nearly every great story has this shape: paradise, fall, redemption. Step through it.

Normal and revolutionary. Small surprises get fixed inside the current story: try another route to the same goal. Large ones break the goal itself, and the whole map has to be redrawn. Peterson borrows the distinction from the historian of science Thomas Kuhn.
